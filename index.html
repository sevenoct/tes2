Design & Build of a Star-Delta Starting and Dynamic Braking System for a 3-Phase Induction Motor Using a PLC

A PLC-based motor control system that softens the starting current with star-delta switching, then stops the motor quickly and safely by injecting DC into the stator, all sequenced automatically with timers and interlocks.

Background

Two weaknesses of the induction motor

Three-phase induction motors are rugged, simple and cheap to maintain, but they are hard on the grid when starting and slow to stop. Both problems are addressed here in one PLC-controlled system.

Starting

At start-up the motor can draw 5–7× its rated current. This can cause voltage dips, heat the winding insulation and shorten motor life when repeated. Star-delta starting begins in star (reduced voltage) and switches to delta once the motor is up to speed.

Stopping

Coasting relies only on friction and inertia, so stopping takes long and is uncontrolled. Injecting DC current into the stator creates a stationary magnetic field that opposes rotor rotation and converts kinetic energy into heat.

The gap

Star-delta and dynamic braking are each common in industry, but combining both under a single PLC control system is still rarely covered in educational settings.

Problem statement

  • How long does the motor take to stop under braking?
  • Which key indicators confirm a safe star-to-delta transition?
  • How can star-delta and dynamic braking logic be implemented in a PLC program that runs automatically and in sequence?
  • How can starting current, transition time and braking time be measured and analysed?

Scope limits

  • Star-delta starting and dynamic braking with a PLC as the central controller only
  • Three-phase induction motors only (no single-phase or DC motors)
  • DC injected into the stator through a diode rectifier circuit
  • Performance tests run under no-load conditions

Objectives

  • Explain how star-delta starting and dynamic braking work in a 3-phase induction motor
  • Apply a PLC as the main controller for starting, transition to delta and braking, automatically and safely

Expected benefits

  • More stable operation: starting and stopping run in the right order without overstressing the motor
  • Fewer operational faults and longer motor life through more regular, precise, easily monitored control

3-phase AC supply→MCB / MCCB→Main contactor→Star / Delta contactors→Motor

Design

Power, control and hardware

1

Power circuit

Three-phase supply passes through a 3-phase MCB and MCCB to the main contactor (MC MAIN). Star (MC STAR) and delta (MC DELTA) contactors reconfigure the motor windings. A thermal overload relay sits in series with the motor, and an ammeter with a selector switch monitors the R, S and T phase currents.

2

Control circuit

A Schneider TM221C16R PLC, powered from a 24 V DC supply, reads the Start, Stop and Emergency Stop inputs and drives four contactor coils. Timer T1 sets the star-to-delta changeover, timer T2 sets the braking duration, and interlocks prevent contactors from overlapping.

3

Braking circuit

When the braking contactor closes, AC is fed to a diode bridge rectifier. The resulting DC flows into two stator terminals, creating a static magnetic field that produces braking torque. After T2 expires the contactor opens so DC does not keep heating the winding.

4

Mechanical layout

The inside panel has three DIN-rail tiers: MCB and PLC, four contactors with the overload relay, and MCCB with the power supply. Terminal blocks sit at the bottom. The front door carries R/S/T indicator lamps, the ammeter, ON/OFF lamps, an emergency stop and PB ON / PB OFF buttons.

Flowchart walk-through

  1. Initialise: all outputs OFF, wait for Start
  2. Start → KM1 main + KM2 star; timer T1 runs
  3. T1 done → KM2 off, KM3 delta on; motor runs
  4. Stop → KM3 and KM1 off; safety delay
  5. KM4 braking + KM2 on; DC injected via diodes; timer T2 runs
  6. T2 done → KM4 off; motor stopped; end

Components

What the system is built from

PLC

Industrial computer with CPU, memory, I/O modules, power supply and programming device. It runs the ladder logic that sequences the contactors. Chosen model: Schneider Modicon M221 TM221C16R (16 I/O, relay output).

Diode rectifier

Stud diodes (full-wave bridge) convert AC to DC for braking. Selected on forward current, peak inverse voltage and heat dissipation, with a heatsink where needed.

Protection

MCB (overcurrent and short circuit), MCCB, and a thermal overload relay whose bimetal element trips the contactor via its NC auxiliary contact.

Contactors

Four Mitsubishi SN-11 (220 V coil) contactors: main, star, delta and braking. Coil, main contacts and auxiliary contacts (NO latching, NC for indication).

Operator interface

NO green Start button, NC red Stop button, a locking emergency stop mushroom button, and pilot lamps for status and fault indication.

Method & testing

How the system will be validated

Methodology: literature study → design (components, PLC ladder diagram, wiring, budget) → testing → analysis of results with recommendations.

1 · Design realisation

Verify the built panel matches the mechanical, power and control design and PLC logic.

2 · Functional test

Check the start sequence from standstill, timed star-to-delta change and braking activation; each PLC output must energise the correct contactor.

3 · Star-delta starting

Measure starting current in star and after changeover to delta, confirming no excessive current spike or mechanical disturbance.

4 · Dynamic braking

Measure stopping time, braking stability and braking contactor response.

5 · PLC timing

Confirm programmed delays (T1 and T2) match design and cause no contactor overlap.

6 · Reliability & safety

Confirm safe, stable operation over a period of time, including emergency stop behaviour.
